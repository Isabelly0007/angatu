Ancient Rituals

The tomb of Darius I, Naqsh-e Rostam, Iran

Mesopotamia

Funerary rituals in ancient Mesopotamia played an important role in the culture and religion of this people, their civilization thriving between approximately 3500 BC and around 539 BC, in the region that today lies between Iraq, parts of Iran, Syria and Turkey. Throughout its history, Mesopotamia was home to several civilizations, including the Sumerians, Akkadians, Babylonians and Assyrians, each of which contributed their own practices and beliefs related to death and funeral rites.

The Mesopotamians shared a belief in the existence of an afterlife, which was seen as a continuation of life on earth. However, the details of this afterlife varied between different civilizations. Generally, the dead were buried, and tombs were often built with adobe bricks. The deceased were accompanied by their personal belongings, food and drink, believing that these items would be needed on the next journey. Funeral rituals varied depending on the Mesopotamian civilization, but they often included religious ceremonies designed to guarantee a safe passage to the afterlife. Priests and priestesses played essential roles in these rituals. During these ceremonies, hymns and prayers were chanted to invoke divine protection and seek a favorable existence in the afterlife.

The Mesopotamians believed that the gods played a fundamental role in the afterlife, and so offerings and sacrifices were made to appease them and ensure the well-being of the deceased in this new phase. Rulers and prominent figures were often buried in majestic tombs, which could include impressive funerary complexes. These monuments were intended to preserve the memory of the deceased and serve as continuous places of worship. The dead were often accompanied by amulets and talismans designed to protect them in the afterlife. It was believed that the deceased needed continuous care, and family members often made offerings and visited the tombs regularly to ensure the comfort and well-being of their loved ones in the next life.

Persian

Death rituals in ancient Persia, which grew from the first millennium BC, involved various practices and beliefs that highlighted the importance of the deceased's transition to the afterlife. When a death occurred, grieving family members conducted rituals that included mourning and religious ceremonies. These rituals were conceived as a way of paying homage to the deceased and requesting protection for the soul on the journey after death. A distinctive practice among the ancient Persians involved the purification of the bodies of

the deceased, often carried out with fresh water taken from rivers, as part of the process of preparing for burial. They preferred graves over cremation, with tombs varying in size and elaboration according to the social status and resources of the deceased's family. During funeral rituals, offerings of food, drink and other material goods were common, as it was believed that these objects would be needed in the afterlife. A substantial part of the Persian population followed Zoroastrianism, one of the world's first monotheistic religions, founded by Zarathustra (or Zoroaster). For adherents of Zoroastrianism, the predominant burial method was the use of "Towers of Silence," structures where bodies were left in the open air, allowing birds of prey, such as vultures, to consume the flesh. This was done in order to avoid contaminating the land and water, which were considered sacred. The Zoroastrian Persians believed that, after death, the souls of the righteous would go on to paradise, while those of the wicked would face an unfavorable fate. The final judgment played an essential role in their beliefs about life after death.


Hebrews

The rituals of death for the ancient Hebrews, according to Jewish traditions and beliefs, were deeply rooted in the religion and culture of that people. The ancient Hebrews considered burial to be a sacred duty. Cremation was strictly avoided, and bodies were buried in tombs, usually in pits dug in the earth. Rapid burial was a common practice, as Jewish law required the dead to be buried on the same day as their death, whenever possible.

When someone died, family and friends performed mourning rituals that included wailing, crying and, in some situations, tearing their clothes as a sign of grief. Mourning could last seven days, known as "Shivah." During the mourning period, family and friends could offer prayers and recite psalms in honor of the deceased. It was also common to offer food and drink to the mourners. The Jewish burial ceremony, known as "Kevurah," was conducted by a group of people called the "Chevra Kadisha." During this ceremony, the body was washed, dressed in a simple white shroud and placed in a coffin.

There was no tradition of embalming the body, in order to maintain the simplicity and natural decomposition of the body. The memory of the deceased was maintained with the annual observance of "Yahrzeit," which is the anniversary date of their death. On this day, relatives would light a candle in honor of the deceased and often visit his grave. The ancient Hebrews believed in an afterlife, and conduct in earthly life was considered crucial for eternal life. Good deeds and obedience to God's commandments were essential aspects of this belief. Jews were taught to respect the dead and graves, and desecration of a grave was considered a serious offense. These practices and beliefs played a significant role in the lives of the ancient Hebrews and continue to influence Jewish traditions regarding death and mourning to this day.
